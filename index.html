Computational Biologist specialising in automated bioinformatics systems, AI-driven analysis and clinical genomic workflows.

My background began in computer science and mathematics, where early work with genetic algorithms led me toward artificial intelligence and biological applications. I found myself increasingly drawn to problems that required both computational thinking and an understanding of the underlying science.

My first experience in research came at CCAMP, BLiSC, where I worked with experimental data from a microfluidic cell-sorting system. As an intern, I developed a web-based analysis and visualisation platform that allowed researchers without a computational background to work with complex experimental data. It was my first opportunity to build a computational tool around a real scientific workflow.

I later moved into bioscience and AI at Queen Mary University of London, where project-based work exposed me to a much broader range of biological datasets and applications. Working across medical imaging, genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics and machine learning gave me a stronger understanding of both the possibilities and the practical challenges of applying computation to biological systems.

At Dhiti Omics Technologies, I moved into clinical genomics and took on a much broader technical role. Alongside sequencing analysis, I worked with laboratory computing, workflow development and research projects across Illumina and Nanopore data. I helped transform a heavily manual infectious-disease analysis process into an automated metagenomic workflow, reducing analysis time from approximately 12 hours to around 2 hours, while also contributing to AI-assisted variant interpretation and other genomic software projects.

These experiences shaped how I approach computational biology: understand the scientific problem, work within the constraints of the data, and build the computational solution around the actual workflow. Rather than treating computation as a separate layer, I try to understand where it can genuinely improve how biological questions are investigated and answered.

My current work extends this approach into systems biology, cheminformatics and explainable AI. I am exploring how molecular structure, transcriptomic signatures and biological pathways can be connected to study compound-induced oxidative stress. The aim is to move beyond asking whether a model can make a prediction, toward understanding which biological signals and mechanisms contribute to that prediction.
